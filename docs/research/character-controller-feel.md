# Make Boogle's beach bodies land with a splat

Boogle's controller already gets the hard, invisible part right: a dynamic cannon-es body steered toward a camera-relative wish velocity, with near-instant ground acceleration (70 m/s²), weaker air control (14 m/s²), a 0.1 s ledge grace and a 0.15 s jump buffer, all on a client-authoritative body that never touches shared sim state ([src/physics/physics.js](../../src/physics/physics.js), [src/player/input.js](../../src/player/input.js)). What it lacks is the *shape* of motion that the canonical feel literature (Celeste, Kyle Pittman's "Building a Better Jump", the GMTK Platformer Toolkit) treats as essential: its jump is a symmetric 1.6 m parabola with no fall multiplier and no jump cut, and its limbless capsule only bobs, so landings, turns and stops carry no weight on screen. The highest-return work is therefore cheap and local. Reshape the jump with three gravity multipliers. Drive a spring-based "jelly" layer on the avatar for landing squash, takeoff stretch and lean into acceleration. Add ground snapping and a sand-ridge step assist, then give water a surface/dive split. None of this needs networking changes: player movement is client-owned, and remote avatars are already animated from interpolated motion ([src/main.ts](../../src/main.ts)). Full active ragdolls, IK legs and a kinematic rewrite are the wrong trade for a small, readable codebase whose comedy already comes from the ball, the face-plant and loose physics (DESIGN.md principle 10).

## The jump is a 0.84-second symmetric arc, the shape every reference calls floaty

The numbers in the code define the arc exactly. A jump sets `velocity.y = 7.6` under `GRAVITY = 18`, so the player rises **1.60 m in 0.42 s and falls for another 0.42 s** with identical gravity both ways, no matter how long the button is held ([src/constants.ts](../../src/constants.ts), [src/physics/physics.js](../../src/physics/physics.js)). Pittman's GDC talk treats the jump trajectory as the foundation that "all other control feel" rests on and builds arcs from designer inputs like height and distance, extending them to variable-height jumps and fast falls with piecewise parabolas ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1023148/Math-for-Game-Programmers-Building)). Secondary summaries of the talk report its central observation: **a pure parabola feels floaty**, and Mario's fix was raising gravity at the apex ([Traversing Virtual](https://traversingvirtual.blogspot.com/2024/07/designing-platformer-player-controls.html)).

The GMTK Platformer Toolkit publishes a compact, readable version of the whole recipe. Gravity is derived as `g = 2·jumpHeight / timeToApex²`, and launch speed is `sqrt(2·g·jumpHeight)`, **adjusted by the current vertical velocity** so jumps stay consistent off springs. A **downward gravity multiplier** applies once vertical velocity goes negative, and a separate **jump-cutoff multiplier** applies while rising if the button is released ([GMTK, Behind the code](https://gmtk.itch.io/platformer-toolkit/devlog/395523/behind-the-code)). Celeste adds a complementary trick: **half gravity at the peak while jump is held**, which buys time to adjust a landing ([Maddy Thorson, Celeste & Forgiveness](https://maddymakesgames.com/articles/celeste_and_forgiveness/index.html)).

For a slapstick game there is a real counter-argument: hang time is when funny things happen, such as heading the ball or landing on a friend. Celeste's apex trick resolves the tension. Players who hold the button get the hang, players who tap get a quick hop, and everyone falls faster than they rose, which is what makes a landing read as a landing. Two smaller defects matter for Boogle specifically. Because the jump *sets* vertical velocity instead of adding to it, jumping while already rising off a slope or a mushroom throws away the upward momentum that would make chained bounces feel great ([src/adapters/mushroom-garden.js](../../src/adapters/mushroom-garden.js)). And the jump action registers only a `down` handler on the pad, the Space key and the touch button ([src/player/input.js](../../src/player/input.js)), so there is currently no release signal to cut a jump with.

## Boogle's capsule needs springs, and springs are cheap

The current avatar animation is a sine bob, a forward lean of up to 0.16 rad, a 4% walking squash and a constant 1.05 stretch whenever airborne ([src/player/avatars.js](../../src/player/avatars.js)). None of it responds to *events*: a 3 m fall onto sand looks identical to stepping off a pebble, and a sprint reversal produces no visible skid. The best-documented cheap fix is **second-order dynamics**, popularized by t3ssel8r's "Giving Personality to Procedural Animations using Math". Procedural motion lacks the inertia that keyframes give. Replacing `y = x` with a spring that follows its target adds that inertia, controlled by three intuitive parameters ([Recapio digest](https://recapio.com/digest/giving-personality-to-procedural-animations-using-math-by-t3ssel8r)). **Frequency** sets response speed, **damping** sets how fast it settles, and **initial response** above 1 overshoots while a negative value anticipates by moving backward first ([second-order dynamics notes](https://chemicalcrux.github.io/vrc-docs/docs/code/best.crux.animator-dynamics/sources/second-order-dynamics)). The one engineering caveat: the semi-implicit Euler solver blows up when the timestep is too large, so the author derives a minimum safe step and substeps or clamps ([Hackaday](https://hackaday.com/2022/06/30/a-math-based-personality-for-games)). That matters in a browser where `dt` can hit Boogle's 50 ms cap.

Toyful Games, whose Very Very Valet is the closest commercial cousin to Boogle's chunky co-op characters, adds the art-direction lesson. They push **squash and stretch until it looks excessive up close**, because it reads correctly in motion on small screens. They also fake whole states visually: a **skid on direction change**, a **spin on fast stick rotation** and a **landing flourish**, none of which changes the underlying movement ([Toyful Games](https://www.toyfulgames.com/blog/character-animations)). That is exactly Boogle's situation. Phones are a first-class target, and every one of these effects can live in the render layer that DESIGN.md already allows to use floats and `Math.random()`.

Legs are the tempting next step, and the technique is well understood. World-space foot targets step when they stray too far from a predicted landing point, a raycast plants them, and alternating groups keep balance ([80.lv, Godot spider](https://80.lv/articles/ik-driven-procedural-spider-locomotion-in-godot-4-5); [PhilS94](https://www.Github.com/PhilS94/Unity-Procedural-IK-Wall-Walking-Spider)). David Rosen's GDC talk showed fluid, interactive animation from very few keyframes in Overgrowth ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020049/Animation-Bootcamp-An-Indie-Approach)). For a capsule with eyes, though, full IK is overkill. Two stubby foot spheres that use the same "step when stranded" rule and snap to the analytic `groundHeight` would give most of the charm with no skeleton.

## Active ragdolls buy chaos Boogle already gets more cheaply

Gang Beasts, Human: Fall Flat, Peak and Wobbledogs are the obvious slapstick touchstones, but their internals are poorly documented, and what is documented argues for caution. An active ragdoll plays a target pose but constrains it to what physics allows, which handles contact well at a **higher CPU cost** ([Wikipedia, Ragdoll physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragdoll_physics)). The only claim about Gang Beasts' method is an anecdotal forum post calling it "full on ragdoll" driven by forces ([Unity discussions](https://discussions.unity.com/t/how-to-blend-ragdoll-physics-with-animation/734320)). Human: Fall Flat's creator first tried independent stick control of each arm, found it messy and reworked it. He also initially believed the physics made online play impossible ([Annual Pardon interview](https://annualpardon.pages.dev/posts/human-fall-flat-was-originally-designed-to-be-portal-meets-limbo-673184); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human:_Fall_Flat)). Wobbledogs' developer chose physics over animation on a budget and then could not guarantee that a dog would reach a given spot, so the AI had to be redesigned around that ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/behind-the-ai-and-physics-of-i-wobbledogs-i-procedurally-goofy-wobbledogs)). Peak's reviewers praise its ragdoll flops, but no technical write-up exists ([FinalBoss](https://finalboss.io/peak-is-a-cooperative-climbing-game-about-failing)).

The pattern is that ragdoll comedy costs input reliability, CPU and network bandwidth (a pose per limb per player), all of which Boogle protects through its small presence packets and "small and readable" rule. Boogle already has the canned version that delivers the joke: the face-plant. It flops you flat with stars and camera shake for 800 ms and syncs as a single presence field, `kd` ([src/features/impact.js](../../src/features/impact.js)). Extending that vocabulary (a tumble, a bonk, a dizzy wobble) gets ragdoll laughs at the cost of one number per event.

## Dynamic-but-steered is the right body, and it needs ground snapping, not a rewrite

The standard trade-off is stark. Dynamic controllers interact with other physics objects "seamlessly" but "not always predictably", while kinematic ones are predictable but must hand-code every push ([Godot docs](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/3.5/tutorials/physics/kinematic_character_2d.html)). Unity's controller package even offers a hybrid mode that simulates mass ratios for pushing ([Unity](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.charactercontroller@1.1/manual/dynamic-body-interaction.html)). Boogle already sits in the sweet spot. Its body is dynamic, so the ball and other players bounce off it for free, but its horizontal velocity is steered directly with a capped delta, which avoids the "swimmy" force-driven feel developers complain about ([gamedev.net](https://gamedev.net/forums/topic/696521-character-controller-rigidbody-vs-kinematics/)). The floating-capsule controller devised by Toyful and reimplemented for three.js in pmndrs' Ecctrl goes one step further. It holds the body above the ground on a raycast spring-damper, follows platforms, and applies **counter-impulses to whatever you stand on, including a downward shove when you jump off it** ([pmndrs/ecctrl](https://github.com/pmndrs/ecctrl); [Joe Binns](https://joebinns.itch.io/stylised-character-controller)). That last detail is a free slapstick verb for Boogle: jumping off a friend's head should squash them.

What Boogle's body lacks is what kinematic controllers ship by default. Rapier's controller defaults to a **45° climb limit, a 45° slide threshold and snap-to-ground within 0.2 of the character size** ([Rapier slopes guide](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/bevy_plugin/character_controller_slopes/); [Rapier source](https://git.nea.moe/github/rapier.git/plain/src/control/character_controller.rs)). Boogle counts any contact with a normal above 0.55 y (about 57°) as ground and never snaps. At 9.5 m/s sprint speed, cresting a dug sand ridge throws the player into the air, which drops coyote, then ground acceleration, then control. Sometimes that is a great accidental ramp. Usually it is a stutter. Because terrain is a heightfield with an analytic `groundHeight(x, z)`, snapping can be a height lookup with no physics query.

Water follows the same principle: separate states, not a single force. Practitioners split swimming into a **surface-float state and an underwater state**, use a chest-height depth probe to pick between them, and let camera pitch steer only underwater ([Godot forums](https://godotforums.org/d/28047-how-to-do-3rd-person-swimming-diving-3d); [Leadwerks](https://www.leadwerks.com/community/topic/8915-leadwerks-25-implementing-swimming/); [godot4-third-person-controller](https://github.com/selgesel/godot4-third-person-controller)). Boogle's single depth-scaled buoyancy force floats everyone up, but there is no dive. A water jump of 4.5 m/s rises only about 0.56 m, which makes climbing back onto a beach step feel sticky.

## Prioritized recommendations for Boogle's controller

The table orders changes by laughs and responsiveness gained per line of code. All of them are local to the player's own body or render layer, so none touches `src/world/sync.ts` or the deterministic sim. Each serves principle 10 (slapstick over precision) or principle 1 (toys before chores). The numbers are starting points for playtests, not answers.

| # | Change | Where | Concrete starting values |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fall multiplier and jump cut.** In preStep, add `b.force.y -= m·g·(k−1)` with k = 1.7 when `vy < 0` and k = 2.5 while rising after jump release. Add `up` handlers to the Space key, pad A and touch jump that set `me.jumpHeld = false`. Optionally use k = 0.5 near the apex (`|vy| < 1.5`) while held, as in Celeste. | `src/physics/physics.js`, `src/player/input.js` | Keep the 1.6 m max height. Tap hop is about 0.6 m. |
| 2 | **Additive jump.** `vy = max(vy, 0) + 7.6`, capped (for example at 12), instead of `vy = 7.6`, so jumping on a mushroom bounce or an upslope chains. | `src/physics/physics.js` | Cap stops runaway mushroom stacking. |
| 3 | **Spring jelly layer.** Replace the fixed squash and lean with second-order springs on `inner`: squash spring kicked by landing vy (scale.y ≈ 1 − 0.05·\|vy\|, clamped at 0.6), takeoff stretch 1.25, pitch/roll lean toward horizontal *acceleration* (not velocity) so braking rocks back and turning banks, and pupils lagging the head. Substep at ≤ 1/60 s. | `src/player/avatars.js` | Use f ≈ 3–4 Hz, ζ ≈ 0.35–0.5, r ≈ 1.5 for an overshooting wobble. Remotes need a vertical-velocity estimate, because `r.prevPos` deltas currently drop y. |
| 4 | **Separate brake and turn rates, with a fake skid.** Follow the GMTK rule: turn rate when input opposes velocity (dot < 0), decel with no input, accel otherwise. Lower sprint braking slightly so reversals slide about 0.2 s, and emit a sand-fx puff and skid tilt during the slide. | `src/physics/physics.js`, a new feature file | Accel 70, turn 45, decel 40 at sprint; keep 70 at walk speed. |
| 5 | **Ground snap and step assist from `groundHeight`.** If grounded last tick, not jumping, and terrain under the foot sphere is within 0.3 m below while `vy < 2`, pull down (`vy = min(vy, −2)`) and refresh coyote. Skip the snap above a launch speed (for example a 1.5 m/s upward ridge component at sprint) so ramps still launch. If moving into terrain that is 0.15–0.6 m higher just ahead, add a small upward velocity (a mantle "pop", like Celeste's corner correction). | `src/physics/physics.js` | Raise coyote to 0.12 s. GMTK cites about 0.2 s as typical. |
| 6 | **Head-stomp counter-impulse.** When a jump's ground contact is another player's head sphere, publish a small "squashed" presence event, like `kd`, that plays a pancake squash on them. | a new `src/features/` file | 300 ms, render-only for the victim. |
| 7 | **Water states, dive and breach.** Split into surface (current buoyancy) and dive. While swimming, the dig/pile buttons are free because you cannot dig underwater, so map RT or Q to dive (cut buoyancy, push down 3 m/s). On release or at the surface with speed, give a breach jump with `vy = 4.5 + 0.6·upward speed` and a splash. | `src/physics/physics.js`, `src/player/input.js` | Reuses existing pad slots, with no new controls. |
| 8 | **Expand the canned-flop vocabulary.** A sprint into a steep wall or another sprinting player becomes a bonk: a 400 ms backward flop, reusing the face-plant path and its `kd`-style field. A hard landing from above about 12 m/s becomes a splat squash. | `src/features/impact.js` pattern | Immunity window prevents chains. |
| 9 | **Camera vertical damping.** Ease the camera look target's y with `1 − exp(−dt·6)` while airborne so jumps don't bob the whole view, and snap it back on landing. | `src/player/player.js` | Horizontal stays rigid for aiming throws. |

Three things are deliberately left out. A kinematic rewrite would lose free ball shoving. Full IK legs or active ragdolls would add CPU, network state and unpredictable input for comedy the flop events already deliver. Wall jumps and ledge grabs are precision verbs that the reference games use for difficulty, which principle 10 tells Boogle to avoid. The mantle pop in recommendation 5 covers the one ledge case that hurts today: getting out of water and over freshly piled sand.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the problem. Boogle doesn't have a controller-architecture problem; it has a missing-*events* problem. Its body already responds instantly, but the jump has no fast fall, landings have no impact and stops have no skid, so nothing on screen marks the moments where comedy happens. Celeste and GMTK show that the arc's asymmetry is where weight comes from, and Toyful shows that exaggerated, purely visual events (skids, landing flourishes, overshoot) carry a co-op game on small screens. Both levers live entirely in code Boogle's client already owns.

The less obvious implication concerns the multiplayer budget. Ragdoll games pay for their chaos in bandwidth and determinism, and Human: Fall Flat's creator first thought online play was impossible. Boogle can instead sync slapstick as tiny named events (`kd` today, then squash, bonk and splat) and let each client's spring layer perform them. This "events, not poses" approach mirrors the project's own "sync edits, not state" rule. It is probably the scalable path for every future pratfall. The open question is tuning, which only the playtests in `docs/playtests.md` can settle: none of the primary sources publish their spring constants or exact frame windows.
