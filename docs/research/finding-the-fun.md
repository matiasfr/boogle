# Finding the fun: how studios prototype and decide fast

*Research report, 1 November 2026. How studios known for play-first design prototype, playtest and kill ideas: Nintendo (Mario 64, Breath of the Wild, Tears of the Kingdom, Splatoon, Mario Odyssey, Pikmin, Miyamoto's over-the-shoulder test), Valve (Portal, Ambinder's playtest methods), the Experimental Gameplay Project, Vlambeer, Double Fine's Amnesia Fortnight, Supercell, Spry Fox, House House (Untitled Goose Game, Big Walk), Landfall, Jonathan Blow, thatgamecompany, Supergiant, Toby Fox, Mark Rosewater, and the little that exists on AI-assisted prototyping. For the process in [find-the-fun.md](../plans/find-the-fun.md), [boogle-2.md](../proposals/boogle-2.md) ("Next: find the fun") and the [protos page](../../protos/protos.ts). Recommendations only: nothing here is a design decision.*

**Markers.** *Verified*: fetched and read at the source (for slides, the text extracted from the PDF). *Secondary*: a news report, wiki or summary of a talk, not the developer's own words on the page. *Derived*: worked out for Boogle from the sources, not stated by anyone. Nothing could be found on Media Molecule's, Mojang's or Supergiant's prototyping beyond origin stories, so they get one line each.

## Summary

1. The studios that find fun fastest build the **verb before the world**: Mario ran around a test garden for months, Breath of the Wild's systems were proved in a top-down room with no puzzles, Undertale started from its battle system.
2. A good prototype asks **one question about a feeling**, not about technology (Blow); a prototype that answers "no" clearly is a success.
3. **Toy first, then goal** (Experimental Gameplay Project): if the bare mechanic isn't satisfying to fiddle with, a goal won't save it.
4. **Most prototypes should die.** Spry Fox kept about 1 in 3 and shipped about 1 hit in 10; Supercell killed seven of ten in prototype. A high kill rate is the process working.
5. Kills are **gut calls by the people closest to the work**, with one tell: when the team is already thinking about "the other game", it's dead (Supercell). Gates should be a little loose (Cook).
6. **Watch, don't ask.** Valve and Miyamoto both treat what players do as truth and what they say as noise; players spot problems well and fix them badly (Rosewater).
7. **Fresh eyes and expert eyes find different things**: Valve and Miyamoto use people who've never seen the game; Nintendo directors and House House use their own long play to judge feel. Boogle has only the expert, so the process has to make up for it.
8. **Comparing variants side by side** is how feel gets tuned (Nijman's 30 tweaks; Valve's design experiments compare conditions), and it's easier to judge "which is better" than "is this good".
9. Timeboxes work: a week (EGP, Big Walk's first prototype, LittleBigPlanet's pitch), two weeks (Amnesia Fortnight), a month (Content Warning). No source found that more time made a prototype more likely to work.
10. With agents, **building stops being the bottleneck and judging becomes it**; the scarce resource is the owner's attention, so the process should spend it only on prototypes that are ready and on questions only a human can answer.

## Findings by source

### Nintendo

**Mario 64: the garden.** In the 1996 developer interviews, Miyamoto says the team began with a test room where Mario could run, climb slopes and jump on blocks, spent about half its time and energy on movement, animation and physics, and only then built the courses, quickly, reshaping maps in-game around how Mario moved (a fast river became a desert valley because it swept Mario away). He had about ten middle schoolers play the King Bob-omb stage for half a day. Secondary accounts describe the "garden" as a test space he uses on every game. ([shmuplations, 1996 interviews](https://shmuplations.com/mario64/), *verified*; [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super_Mario_64), *secondary*)

**Miyamoto's over-the-shoulder test.** At GDC 2009, Iwata showed a photo of Miyamoto standing behind a player and described the method: hand the controller to someone who knows nothing about the game, explain nothing, and watch for where they get hooked, where they get bored, what they miss. Iwata joked about "random employee kidnapping" for these tests. ([AV Club](https://www.avclub.com/avc-at-gdc-09-day-three-shigeru-miyamoto-does-it-over-1798216100), [MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/business-news/publishing/nintendo-president-details-miyamotos-approach-to-development/), *secondary*)

**Breath of the Wild: the 2D-looking prototype.** At GDC 2017, Fujibayashi, Dohta and Takizawa showed a prototype in the look of the NES Zelda that ran the real physics and "chemistry" engine underneath (Nintendo later said it was a top-down 3D build). It had no puzzles: a river, some trees, and the player finding their own way. The point was to test the systems that became the game's backbone at the lowest possible cost of content. ([Thumbsticks](https://www.thumbsticks.com/gdc-17-breaking-conventions-breath-of-the-wild/), [Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/check-out-this-zelda-breath-of-the-wild-nes-style-prototype/), *secondary*)

**Tears of the Kingdom: prototype from what already exists.** Fujibayashi pitched Ultrahand by building vehicles out of parts already in Breath of the Wild (cog wheels on a board as a car, a paddle steamer, a slab-barrel tank fired with a bomb) and showing them to the team. When the real prototypes of Ultrahand and Fuse arrived, the lead physics engineer expected "chaos"; objects flew off daily. The team's answer was a rule rather than patches: make everything physics-driven, so systems multiply ("create a system that makes fun things happen", Dohta). ([Nintendo Everything on Ask the Developer](https://nintendoeverything.com/zelda-tears-of-the-kingdom-director-used-breath-of-the-wild-to-demonstrate-links-new-abilities-during-early-development/), [Digital Trends on GDC 2024](https://www.digitaltrends.com/computing/how-nintendo-designed-tears-of-the-kingdom-physics/), *verified secondary*)

**Splatoon: 70 ideas, one tofu.** Iwata Asks: the team brainstormed "at least seventy" ideas over about six months; a prototype of white and black tofu blocks shooting ink in a maze, playable four on four, was the one that survived several rounds of presentations. The team had set out to find "a new structure", not a character: the tofu became a rabbit, then a squid, after the mechanic was proved. ([Iwata Asks: Splatoon](https://www.nintendo.com/en-gb/Iwata-Asks/Iwata-Asks-Splatoon/Splatoon/1-It-Started-With-Tofu/1-It-Started-With-Tofu-1021365.html), *verified*)

**Mario Odyssey: fun to imagine, then fun to play.** Motokura describes starting with many prototypes, of which "not everything prototyped would make it into the final game", and a two-step filter: is the idea fun to imagine, and is it also fun to play with? Capture was the idea that tied the abilities together. ([GeekDad interview](https://geekdad.com/2017/10/super-mario-odyssey-developer-interview/), *verified*; [Time](https://time.com/4997917/super-mario-odyssey-nintendo-switch/), *verified*)

**Pikmin.** Nintendo's own account is that the team's scattered ideas were drawn into one game-flow diagram by Miyamoto, and only one stage was finished when it was shown at E3 2001. The well-known "ants in the garden" story is Miyamoto's own framing but he calls the reality more complicated. Little else on its prototyping was found. ([Ask the Developer: Pikmin 4](https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/ask-the-developer-vol-10-pikmin-4-part-1/), *secondary*)

### Valve

**Portal: test from week one.** Narbacular Drop's student team tested only in its final month and had no time to fix what they found. At Valve, they ran a rough first room in their first week, hacking portals out of existing teleport and camera code, and playtested weekly or whenever there was new content, with people who had never played it, told to play as they would at home, watched start to finish. Playtests split a puzzle testers struggled with into three steps, turned unexpected solutions into new puzzles, and showed the game went "a little dry" 15 to 30 minutes in, which led to GLaDOS's narration. ([Thinking with Portals](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/thinking-with-portals-creating-valve-s-new-ip), *verified*)

**Ambinder's playtest methods (GDC 2009).** The slides frame design as hypotheses and playtests as experiments; the goal of a playtest is fun, not bugs or balance and "definitely not focus testing". Direct observation is the core: watch behaviour, simulate the at-home experience, have a design goal; its risks are observer bias and over-reading one salient moment. Verbal reports and Q&As are flagged as biased ("people don't know why they do what they do"; group anchoring). Design experiments compare two or more conditions. Stats see trends but "averages hide extreme examples" and miss context. ([slides](https://cdn.cloudflare.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2009/GDC2009_ValvesApproachToPlaytesting.pdf), *verified*)

**The cadence and the observers.** Mark Brown's survey of Valve's process: test on Friday, discuss Monday, change, test again; observers stay silent; feedback is data to interpret, not orders; small cabals own a slice and watch their own work fail. He notes this suits linear, hand-built games and a studio with Valve's resources. ([GMTK](https://gmtk.substack.com/p/valves-secret-weapon), *secondary*)

### Prototyping practice

**Experimental Gameplay Project (Gabler, Gray, Kucic, Shodhan, 2005).** One person, under seven days, a shared theme. Lessons: build the **toy** first (no win or lose, just a fun thing to play with), then add a goal that grows out of it; recognise dead ends and cut losses rather than salvage code; "make it juicy"; complexity isn't needed; theming can't rescue a bad mechanic; nobody cares how you made it. They found no link between time spent and success, and that their good games were predictably fun before any code was written. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-to-prototype-a-game-in-under-7-days), *verified*)

**Jonathan Blow.** At the 2007 Independent Games Summit he argued a prototype must test the feel of the core idea, not the technology: a gesture-combat test worked but "didn't exercise the game play parts". Oracle Billiards (seeing where balls will end up) wasn't fun but was a successful prototype, because it gave him a feeling worth following; that question moved into a platformer and became Braid. A prototype can be worth it for what it teaches even if it isn't fun. He urged against ideas that are only "good enough". ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-prototyping-for-indie-developers), *verified*) Later he described Braid as designed by discovery: ask "what if?" and let the system answer. ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/blow-braid-designed-itself/), *secondary*)

**Rami Ismail (Vlambeer).** Prototyping is research: "problem-finding", not problem-solving; fail fast, keep cycles short; if a project is stuck in prototyping, "now is a very good time to cut your losses", having lost a month or two and gained knowledge. ([Levelling the Playing Field: Milestones](https://ltpf.ramiismail.com/milestones/), *verified*) Elsewhere he warns that studios can spend a year before noticing a project isn't moving. ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/rami-ismail-s-ten-essential-tips-for-thriving-in-the-indie-game-industry), *secondary*) His co-founder Jan Willem Nijman's talk "The art of screenshake" takes a dull shooter and applies 30 small tweaks one at a time, each visibly better than the last: feel found by comparison, step by step. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/vlambeer-co-founder-shares-advice-on-building-better-action-games), *secondary*)

**Double Fine's Amnesia Fortnight.** A recurring studio-wide jam: the studio forgets its main project for two weeks and splits into small teams, each building a game from a pitch. It produced Costume Quest, Stacking, Iron Brigade and others, and carried the studio after Brütal Legend. In 2012, 23 pitches went to a public vote; leadership staffed five teams within 48 hours. Lessons the team drew: broad briefs (Spacebase) must commit to one specific version fast; asking why people didn't pick a concept teaches as much as why they did. ([Game Developer, 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/amnesia-fortnight-how-double-fine-embraces-the-future), *verified*; [Wikipedia: Costume Quest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Costume_Quest), *secondary*)

**Landfall.** TABS began as a physics experiment during Clustertruck and became a game at a week-long jam in a castle; Content Warning was built by five people mostly during a month-long internal jam in Seoul. ([TABS press kit](https://landfall.se/tabs-press-kit), [Content Warning press kit](https://landfall.se/content-warning-press-kit), *secondary*)

**Media Molecule, Mojang, Toby Fox.** LittleBigPlanet's first pitch to Sony was a 2D physics prototype (Craftworld), reportedly built in about a week ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LittleBigPlanet_(2008_video_game)), fan wikis, *secondary*). Minecraft's "Cave Game" tech test took about a week and went to forum testers immediately ([TCRF](https://tcrf.net/Proto:Minecraft:_Java_Edition/Pre-Classic/Cave_Game), *secondary*). Undertale's battle system came before its story, and a public demo of just that system went out with the Kickstarter ([Undertale Wiki](https://undertale.wiki/w/Undertale), [The Mary Sue](https://www.themarysue.com/interview-undertale-game-creator-toby-fox/), *secondary*). Supergiant designed Hades for early access from day one, shipping a small modular version and growing it with players ([OnlySP](https://onlysp.escapistmagazine.com/greg-kasavin-hades-interview/), *secondary*); no account of their pre-production prototypes was found.

### Deciding: kill rates and kill calls

**Spry Fox (Daniel Cook).** Over five years: 31 projects started as prototypes, 20 didn't pan out, 11 released, of which 3 were real successes; roughly 1 in 10 prototypes became a hit. Cook's advice is to budget for expected failure: "by disproving bad ideas early, we put real money into games" with better odds. ([Minimum Sustainable Success](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/minimum-sustainable-success), *verified*) In a podcast he puts the odds of even a well-executed idea at one in five to one in ten; prototypes run two days to five weeks; a stage-gate funnel with example criteria like "engaging for at least 15 minutes" and whether the team is improving the fun fast enough ("prototype momentum"); gates kept "a little loose"; two or three alternatives held in mind so a kill never stalls the team; dig into testers' vague doubts. ([Game Thinking podcast #104](https://gamethinking.io/podcast/104-dan-cook/), *verified*)

**Supercell.** Of its ten games before the talk, seven were killed in prototype, two in soft launch, one shipped (Clash Royale). No checklist: metrics count, but the call is a gut feeling, made by the team, not executives. The tell: once you're thinking about the game you "should" be making, you've already killed this one. The question: can we fix it? Dissent is answered with "prove me wrong". Dower warns this doesn't suit every game: Rocket League took seven years. ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/maybe-it-s-time-to-kill-your-game-and-move-on-supercell-on-cutting-its-losses), *verified*) The company marks kills with champagne. ([PocketGamer.biz](https://www.pocketgamer.biz/supercell-opens-up-on-cancelled-projects-and-the-role-of-failure-in-creativity/), *secondary*)

**Mark Rosewater (Magic: The Gathering), lesson 19 of 20:** your audience is good at recognising problems and bad at solving them. Treat a player's suggestion as a symptom, not a prescription. ([talk notes](https://notes.hamatti.org/sources/talks/'magic-the-gathering'-20-years,-20-lessons-learned-by-mark-rosewater), *secondary*)

### House House

**Untitled Goose Game.** The studio's first prototype, about moving a little person through a space, "didn't completely pan out" and was dropped. The goose started as a joke photo; once a goose and one person shared a space, "it immediately created a relationship". The honk was in within days, before it did anything: being a goose came first, goals later. Behaviour was added one piece at a time to one AI character with no up-front plan, and they filled in whatever players believed should work. ([Panic Podcast](https://podcast.panic.com/episodes/s01e01/transcript/), [Behind the HONK](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behind-the-honk-an-i-untitled-goose-game-i-q-a), *verified*)

**Big Walk.** Started as a one-week lockdown experiment: a large empty photo-scanned terrain with proximity voice, friends walking and trying to stay together. The discovery was that "the scale of the interaction" (waving and gesturing at close range) was the revelation; they were "chasing a feeling". They later made about 15 challenges in one week and kept roughly half; a door gauntlet was cut as un-fun, but some bad designs were "bad in ways that were interesting". They describe the game as heavily informed by playtests, watching hours of footage, and found scaling a challenge down to fewer players harder than up. ([Kotaku interview](https://kotaku.com/the-creators-of-big-walk-break-down-the-goty-contenders-development-puzzles-and-stunning-ending-2000725133), *verified*) The project then ran six years. ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Walk), *secondary*)

### thatgamecompany

**Journey.** Chen's team removed weapons, the HUD, lobby and names, and changed single-use items to respawn for the second player, because shared resources had created competition; an early prototype where players distracted monsters for each other was cut for readability. Playtests settled a long debate about the ending: players kept walking toward the end expecting something there. ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/development-news/jenova-chen-reveals-secrets-of-journey-design/), *verified*; [GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/an-interview-with-jenova-chen-how-journeys-creator-went-bankrupt-and-won-game-of-the-year/4/), *secondary*)

### Expert players versus fresh players

Valve and Miyamoto lean on people who've never seen the game ("Kleenex testers", used once). Industry usage also names developer testers, who see past the rough edges of an early build and so are used early. ([Video Game Buzzwords](https://videogamebuzzwords.fandom.com/wiki/Kleenex_Testing), *secondary*) Sakurai's HAL tested early Smash with staff's children and friends in a party atmosphere, partly for morale. ([Automaton](https://automaton-media.com/en/news/super-smash-bros-was-playtested-in-a-way-thats-not-possible-today-sakurai-reveals/), *secondary*) No study comparing the two was found. *Derived:* the sources agree that an expert is the right judge of feel and of whether a toy is worth pursuing, and the wrong judge of discoverability, readability and onboarding, which only a fresh player reveals.

### AI-assisted and agentic development

Evidence is thin and mostly anecdotal. Wharton interviewed 20 studios; the three "AI-first" ones described a collapse in handoffs from idea to prototype to playtest and a vertical slice falling from about four months to four weeks, but gave no prototype counts and said human coordination "cannot be automated". ([Wharton Generative AI Labs](https://gail.wharton.upenn.edu/research-and-insights/beyond-copy-paste/), *verified*, small sample) Solo developers running five or six coding agents in parallel report that coordination, not coding, is the bottleneck ([DEV](https://dev.to/maxxmini/i-built-a-full-game-in-one-day-using-ai-agents-heres-what-happened-3c3o), [DEV](https://dev.to/yurukusa/i-ran-a-5-agent-game-studio-with-claude-code-teams-2lpk), *secondary*). One studio found agent-driven end-to-end playtesting too slow and unstable while the game was changing (Luden.io, *secondary*, page not fetchable). Pieter Levels' 2025 Vibe Jam (80% AI-written, playable instantly on the web, no logins) drew hundreds of entries; critics said most remixed rather than invented. ([Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/tech/pieter-levels-just-announced-the-winners-of-the-2025-vibe-code-game-jam-Uz0wHG4pI3KBOiFhP5YR), *secondary*) No GDC talk or postmortem on a studio running many agent-built gameplay prototypes in parallel was found. *Derived:* every pre-AI source assumes building is expensive and judging is cheap; with agents that flips, so the classic advice about cheap prototypes applies with more force while the advice about protecting the judge's time becomes the main constraint.

## What this means for Boogle

Boogle's setup today: one owner, an experienced player with little time, playtests solo on an iPad; about 20 agents build greybox prototypes behind `?proto=`, with 2–3 `&v=` variants; [the protos page](../../protos/protos.ts) lists 8 entries with one gut question each; findings go to [playtests.md](../playtests.md). Much of the research already matches this (solo first, mechanics only, one question, no twice → cut). The recommendations below are about the gaps.

1. **Put the bare verb in front of the owner before any loop.** Mario's garden, BotW's puzzle-free room and the goose honking before it meant anything all test the verb alone. For each step in find-the-fun, offer a "toy" variant with no work site, cost or counter at all (carry logs around; ride the wagon on a loop that's already laid) beside the variant with the loop, so the owner can tell whether a "no" is about the verb or the goal.
2. **Write each gut question about a feeling, and make "no" a good result.** Blow's Oracle Billiards and Big Walk's "bad in interesting ways" challenge were successes because they taught something. On the protos page, record a "no" with one line on *what* felt wrong (as the haul entry already did: "tall stack shouldn't sway") and count it as a finished prototype, not a failed one.
3. **Budget for most prototypes to die, and say so.** Spry Fox kept about a third, Supercell killed seven in ten. Add a `retired` reason to `protos.ts` (cut, merged, superseded) and expect most entries to end there; a page where everything stays live is a sign the bar is too low.
4. **Use Supercell's tell as the kill test.** After a sitting, the owner's only extra question: "while playing this, was I thinking about a different version?" If yes, the next prototype is that version; the current one retires. That fits an expert's fast gut better than any checklist.
5. **Make variants comparisons, not menus.** Judging "which of these two" is easier and more reliable than "is this good" (Nijman's one-tweak-at-a-time, Ambinder's design experiments). Keep variants to two or three that differ in **one** thing, give each a plain-language difference on its button, and ask the entry as "which won, and by how much" (a clear win, a slight edge, can't tell). "Can't tell" is a finding: that parameter doesn't matter; pick the cheaper one.
6. **Let the owner switch variants without reloading.** In the spirit of Nijman's live toggles and the haul kit's tuning panel, a variant switcher inside the prototype (a key or a corner button) lets him compare in the same spot within seconds, rather than across page loads where memory of the feel fades. *Derived.*
7. **Timebox each prototype and its iterations.** EGP's week, Amnesia Fortnight's two, Ismail's "a month or two then cut". Give each prototype a build budget (say one agent session plus at most two feel passes), and if it hasn't earned a "yes" by its second playtest, retire it. This already exists as "no twice → change it or cut it"; make it visible on the page as a playtest count per entry.
8. **Ration the owner's attention: the protos page is the bottleneck, so gate what reaches it.** With agents, building is cheap and judging is expensive (the AI sources and Supercell's "team decides" both point here). Only list a prototype as live once its builder has looked at its own `/look` motion sheet and played it through on an iPad-sized viewport. Show at most a handful of live entries at once, ordered by which question unblocks the most work.
9. **Keep sittings short and the first minute decisive.** The owner judges fast; Cook's "15 minutes of engagement" is a ceiling, not a target, for a greybox. Each prototype should put the owner into the verb within a few seconds of loading (spawned next to the thing, no walk), so a sitting of several prototypes fits a short break.
10. **Record behaviour, not only verdicts.** Valve and Miyamoto trust what players do over what they say. Have each prototype log a few counters to the console or the copyable entry (rides taken, seconds spent, times a variant was switched to), so "rode it four times" sits beside "felt fine". The owner's own "I kept doing X" is the most valuable line in an entry.
11. **Treat the owner's suggestions as symptoms.** Rosewater's lesson applies to owners as much as players. When an entry says "make it Y", the agent picking it up should first restate the problem behind it and consider one alternative fix beside the literal one, offered as a variant.
12. **Add a fresh player occasionally, for different questions.** The owner is the right judge of feel; he can't judge whether a newcomer understands what to do. Once a step passes, one silent over-the-shoulder session with someone who has never seen Boogle (a friend on the iPad, the owner watching without explaining) answers discoverability. Keep these off the critical path and log them in playtests.md separately.
13. **Prove systems with what exists before building new parts.** Fujibayashi pitched Ultrahand with Breath of the Wild's existing parts; Valve hacked portals out of existing teleports. Before an agent builds a new system for a question, check whether a variant built from current pieces (the ball, the wagon, the platform carry, haul bundles) can answer it.
14. **Hold two or three alternative directions per step.** Cook keeps alternatives in mind so a kill never stalls the team; Splatoon had 70 ideas for one survivor. For each find-the-fun step, keep a short list of the next prototype to try if the current one gets a "no", so a cut turns into a new build the same day.
15. **Run the occasional amnesia week.** Double Fine's and Landfall's best games came out of short, separate jams, not the main plan. Now and then, give a batch of agents one-week "toys" around a theme from the owner's likes (vehicles, construction you can watch, goofiness), outside the find-the-fun order, and put them on the page under `depth`.
16. **Don't let polish answer the question.** EGP: theming can't rescue a bad mechanic; a vertical slice can hide one. Keep the "mechanics only" rule from find-the-fun.md and add its mirror: no feel or art pass on a prototype until it has had a "yes" on its gut question, so a nice look never stands in for a fun verb.
