# Research: how systems fit together (Big Walk and others), applied to Boogle

*Research report, 30 October 2026. Follows `docs/research/big-walk-and-communal-endings.md`; mechanics only, no NPC or story beats. Tags: **[V]** fetched and read this round; **[S]** secondary (search snippet, fan guide or paraphrase). Big Walk is three months old: its detail comes from reviews, Wikipedia and fan guides, which disagree on names.*

---

## 1. Big Walk: how the pieces interlock

### 1.1 Short voice range makes the tools necessary

- Wikipedia [V]: voices become "inaudible when a short distance away", "meaning that players can become easily separated from one another". Out of range they use "hand signals", "leaving messages on whiteboards", and "flashlights, laser pointers, megaphones, walkie-talkies, static broadcast stations, and flare guns".
- Disseldorp to Kotaku [V]: "whenever you can hear something, it's really obvious that other people will be able to hear it too."
- Tools lie in the world. A fan guide [V, allthings.how]: they "are not permanent upgrades tucked in a menu". Walkie-talkies sit "below the Red Tower" and "only work in pairs"; flare guns turn up "near puzzle solution areas".
- **Carrying is the constraint.** Game Informer [V]: "you can initially only hold one thing in your hands at a time, equipment comes at a premium." A backpack worn by a teammate adds a slot [V, guide].
- **Night couples tools to the clock.** Game Informer [V]: "A flashlight might be useless during the day, but at night you need one".

### 1.2 Landscape and traversal

- Terrain shapes sound. Disseldorp [V, Kotaku]: "you can climb up on the top of a hill and echo across a valley."
- Game Informer [V]: "Going anywhere takes time; not a gameplay resource or number on a meter, but just real-life time." Sitting "sends you sliding down a hill".

### 1.3 Folk-game challenges built from the communication limits

- Each challenge removes one channel and leaves others. Guide examples [V, allthings.how]: in the Orange Barn "text and voice chat are blocked by the barrier", so a trapped player gestures; in the Beach House "You need a player in each house", and without the telescope "the only other way to pass the number is a walkie-talkie".
- Game Informer [V]: "Soundproof rooms forced our team to figure out how to communicate nonverbally."
- Gillespie-Cook [V, Kotaku]: "what is the cooperation you can have that doesn't feel like either one person is just in charge of everything."

### 1.4 Progression: rewards make keys, and keys open travel

Fan guides agree on the structure. Names differ: "Heads" (Whisper of the House), "gourds" (Game Informer), "blorbs" (search snippets). Wikipedia calls it "a purposely unnamed bulbous object", red for easier challenges and purple for harder ones [V].

1. Every challenge pays one reward. **Each coloured tower** takes five (the tutorial four) and gives a blank key. Whisper of the House [V]: "Heads also are not locked to the color of the nearest tower"; towers "do not have to be completed in exactly that order."
2. **The key-cutter chain:** the blank key visits five cutter stations across the land, following arrows. The guide's roles: "one player carries the key; one player keeps the route lit; one player watches the arrow" [V]. The chain is itself a walk.
3. **Each key opens transport or information, not a level:**

| Tower | Opens | What it changes |
|---|---|---|
| Red | Map room | Portable map, compass, laser pointer; "a decision point, not another puzzle destination" [V, Whisper] |
| Green | Chairlift | "links you toward the Blue, Yellow, and Black Tower regions with far less walking" [V, allthings.how] |
| Yellow | Tunnel network | [V, Whisper] |
| Blue | Train | "Three Train carriages begin circulating on the rail loop"; "the carriages can be used to reconnect players who have split between regions" [V, Whisper] |
| Black | Black Key → the Wall → the Big Goodbye | Wikipedia [V] |

Game Informer [V]: "Each one introduces a new feature that recontextualizes a piece of the island".

**The structure is a loop:** challenges limit communication → rewards build keys → key chains are walks → keys open transport and maps → a split group covers more ground and regroups → more challenges.

### 1.5 Players in different places

- Game Informer [V]: "It provides tools for you to venture apart, to go vast distances, and still reconnect". Challenges scale "from two to three, to four-plus".
- Disseldorp [V, Kotaku]: "a couple of really far-away synchronized button presses ... provided everyone brings a clock." Gillespie-Cook: "being alone, but knowing that someone else is somewhere in the world doing stuff."

## 2. Untitled Goose Game: goals that read the systems

- The lists came last. House House to Game Developer [V]: "Our approach to them was just to think of interesting things you could already do in the game."
- The systems were "a mix between systemic stuff that worked for everything once it was set up" and "handcrafted solutions where we tried to anticipate what else people might try" [V].
- Disseldorp to Nintendo [V, via Nintendo Everything]: "giving the player a 'to do' list would be a good way for them to find all the little things we put in."
- Disseldorp on the Panic podcast [V]: "Objects, are kind of the language that is used for the goose to interact with the people in the game." Strasser: "the honk eventually had all these sort of game play ramifications."

**What this means:** a to-do item names a result, not a method. Any route through the systems that reaches it counts, and the list works as a tour of what the systems can do.

## 3. Other games known for systems that fit

**Breath of the Wild and Tears of the Kingdom.** BotW adds a rule layer between elements and materials alongside the physics. Dohta [V, Thumbsticks, GDC 2017]: "Aside from rigid body dynamics we consider all phenomena that occur in nature to be chemistry", so that puzzles "that reference the way the real world works can help players solve them instinctively". Fujibayashi on TotK [S, Polygon via Zelda Dungeon]: "This idea of multiplicative gameplay really adds a lot of factors to the equation" and "Keep it simple and to the point."

**Outer Wilds.** Nothing is locked: every place can be reached from the start, and what you learn in one place opens up another. Beachum [V, Game Developer]: "knowledge being the only real thing of value in this world"; "the only thing you can bring back in time with you is your memories". Explicit rewards were dropped: "collectibles or upgrades, explicit, tangible forms of progress". Places connect because "Everything is on its own clock". Events on one planet are scheduled, so they can be seen from another.

**A Short Hike.** Golden feathers set stamina for "running, climbing, or performing midair jumps" [V, Wikipedia]. Feathers are found by exploring, so exploring makes you a better explorer. Robinson-Yu [V, PlayStation Blog]: "To climb into the next region, the player will need a specific amount of stamina ... However, I wanted to make sure it was also possible to get up there with barely any golden feathers too." The map does the pacing: "Each region would be elevated a different height above sea-level". "I just started sculpting areas that were fun to climb and glide between."

**From Dust.** Rock, soil, water, lava and plants each follow simple rules and share one terrain. Chahi [V, Game Developer]: "It is very simple rules, and these rules -- there is a kind of convergence". The cycles are readable: "the player can anticipate the process"; "you can have a cycle of eruption, plant coming, and then it's destroyed".

**Noita.** Every pixel follows the material rules. Purho [V, Game Developer]: "It functions as this emergence-generating machinery that keeps on generating these interesting situations of play", and players learn "to explore as a set of systems."

**Townscaper.** One input, and the algorithm resolves everything near it. The player places a block, and "the rest of the existing structure" re-evaluates [V, Game Developer, Tommy Thompson]. Stålberg: "I want the user to feel like they're in control of the large shapes"; "it is actually allowed to fail silently." Also on the split of work [S, PC Gamer]: "how clear the division of labor between the user and the algorithm is."

**Teardown.** Because everything can be destroyed, there are no locked doors, so the challenge becomes efficiency. Gustafsson [V, Game Developer]: the game keeps "the entire map accessible at all times", with the challenge in "finding efficient shortcuts instead"; it has "a relaxed sandbox phase for planning and preparation and an action-packed execution phase."

## 4. Seven principles of system cohesion

1. **One shared medium that everything reads.** BotW's chemistry, From Dust's terrain and Noita's pixels each give the world one layer of state that every object reacts to. In Boogle that layer is height and water.
2. **A limit on one system creates a use for another.** Big Walk's short voice range is what gives the walkie, flare, megaphone and whiteboard a purpose. Carrying one item at a time and night darkness make you choose between them.
3. **Unlocks change how you move, not what exists.** Big Walk's keys open a map, a chairlift, tunnels and a train. Outer Wilds unlocks only knowledge, and A Short Hike only stamina. In each, the world stays the same and you get around it differently.
4. **One resource, many verbs.** One stamina pool covers running, climbing and jumping, and exploring refills it (A Short Hike).
5. **Goals come after, and they name results.** Goose wrote its lists from "things you could already do". A goal that names an outcome can be reached by any route through the systems.
6. **Readable, anticipatable rules.** Players should be able to predict what happens: From Dust's cycles, BotW's real-world logic, Townscaper failing silently.
7. **Distance is a material, and travel reconnects.** Big Walk uses split groups on purpose, and its train exists partly to bring them back together. Teardown removes barriers and asks for efficiency instead.

### 5.2 Five mechanics that add connections, not content

**1. Standing water waters seeds.** A buried seed counts as watered when its cell holds sim water (say 3 cm for a few ticks). A dug channel becomes irrigation; a ball splash waters seeds round a pool; rain and tide will matter later. *Links:* water, sand, ball, mushrooms. *Sync:* **Lockstep**; the mushrooms tick reads water through a sibling rule. *Smallest test:* a unit test that plants a seed, sets its cell's water to 10 cm, runs 120 ticks and sees it grow while a dry control stays at 0; plus an `edits` case in `world-replay.test.ts`. A changed tick rule diverges across mixed builds, so check how the hash resync handles it first.

**2. Things that land in water float and drift.** Today a water landing is lost (`y = -3`). Instead the item drifts with the current until it beaches, and a seed plants where it beaches. You can send a present to a friend down a channel. *Links:* throwing, water, channels, gifts, wash-ups, later rafts. *Sync:* **Lockstep**: a gifts tick steps floating items one cell along the integer water gradient. The state shape changes, so `version` + `migrate`. *Smallest test:* a unit test on a sloped water strip. The item moves downstream within N ticks and stays put in still water. The item count is conserved, and two runs give the same hash.

**3. Wind from the clock.** A pure function of tick and seed (lookup table, no trig), telegraphed by sail speed, gull drift and clouds. It pushes the frisbee and the ball, and later the land yacht. *Links:* windmills (finally a job), frisbee, ball, gulls, vehicles. *Sync:* **Lockstep without state**, like the planned tide, so every tab replays the same frisbee flight; consumers stay Owner (ball) and Local (flight). *Smallest test:* a unit test that `windAt(tick, seed)` is deterministic, bounded and continuous; then a scenario where one frisbee throw at two ticks lands in two places.

**4. Mushroom caps glow at night.** Emissive caps (no new lights) below daylight 0.35, the fireflies' threshold. A planted row becomes a lit path, a night bounce marks where you are (Big Walk's flare), and friends find your traces. *Links:* mushrooms, day and night, communication, caves. *Sync:* **Local**, reading lockstep mushroom state. *Smallest test:* a `look` night still of the garden, and `npm run perf` before and after.

**5. A timetabled train that carries whatever rests on it.** Position is a pure function of tick around a loop, stopping at home, the lighthouse, the windmills and the mine (which already has rails and a minecart). Wagons are kinematic bodies like the Boogler, so players, the ball, items and he can ride. Its horn is a honk note that can complete a chord. *Links:* every landmark, ball, throwing, honk, the clock, regrouping split players (Big Walk's train). *Sync:* **Lockstep without state** for the train; riders keep their kinds (Owner). Not Host, which would restart with each host. *Smallest test:* a unit test that `trainAt(tick)` is continuous across the loop's wrap and stops in each station's window; then a scenario where a ball on a wagon moves at least 20 m.

**Order:** 1 and 2 first: they close the core loop (water, sand, farm) and add nothing to place. Then 3, which gives the mainland a job. The train is the biggest build, and every other system feeds it.
