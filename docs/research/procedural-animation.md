# Procedural animation: what to do in code, what to author as clips

*Research report, 1 November 2026. How shipped games and technical writers animate characters, props, construction, vehicles and crowds in code (springs, secondary motion, procedural steps, ragdolls, squash and stretch, tweens, vertex animation, vertex animation textures), and where authored clips win, read against Boogle's capsule avatars, the `?proto=build` hut, the wagon and soapbox prototypes and WebGPU three.js 0.187 on an M4 iPad Pro. Builds on [character-controller-feel.md](character-controller-feel.md) (jump shape, the spring jelly layer, why not active ragdolls), [stylized-rendering-on-a-budget.md](stylized-rendering-on-a-budget.md) (vertex wind), [creature-ai-and-ecosystems.md](creature-ai-and-ecosystems.md) (instanced critters) and [progression-feel-exploration.md](progression-feel-exploration.md) (cannon-es suspension maths) without repeating them. Several GDC talks are video only: where I read a listing or a secondary summary rather than the talk, the text says so. `docs/plans/asset-pipeline.md` was not on `main` when this was written.*

## Summary

1. Boogle already animates almost everything in code, and every file rolls its own maths: `feel.ts` has an explicit-Euler squash spring, the soapbox has a second spring, the build prototype has a hand-written quadratic drop. A shared module of exact, frame-rate-independent dampers and springs ([Holden](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)) plus a handful of easing curves is the cheapest upgrade and the base for everything below.
2. Code wins for anything driven by gameplay numbers that change every frame: squash, lean, look-at, wheel spin, suspension, chassis roll, construction pieces, wind, fish and gull motion. Clips win for the performance of a specific gesture (a wave, a celebration, the Boogler's acting) and for anything an artist must judge pose by pose.
3. Overgrowth's character ran on a handful of key poses blended by physics-driven code ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020583/Animation-Bootcamp-An-Indie-Approach)); Spore animated creatures nobody had seen in advance with IK "animation objects" stored relative to the body ([SIGGRAPH 2008](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/216813518_Real-time_motion_retargeting_to_highly_varied_user-created_morphologies)). Both say: author few poses, let code place them.
4. Stubby feet are the next avatar step: two foot objects that step to a raycast point ahead, on an arc, scaled by speed, with no skeleton ([Baluchon](https://piecesgames.itch.io/baluchon/devlog/193685/procedural-walking-system)). The prior report already rejected full IK legs and active ragdolls; the TABS write-up confirms why (unplayable outside its genre).
5. Squash should preserve volume (stretch `s` on one axis, `1/√s` on the other two), which `feel.ts` approximates with `1 + sq·0.5`. Velocity skinning shows the same squash and floppiness can run per vertex on the GPU ([Rohmer et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04934)).
6. Construction you watch is a tween problem, not a clip problem: a blueprint of pieces, each with a start time, a fall curve, an overshoot settle and a puff. City builders that bake build-ups as frames do it to save art time, not because it looks better ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/city-builder-games-make-build-up-animations-cheaply)). The hut already works this way; it needs the shared easing curves and a few more verbs (fly in on an arc, wobble, scaffold wobble when walked on).
7. Vertex animation in TSL (`positionNode`, `patchNode`) is the budget path for wind, wobble and critters: sample one noise field per instance at its anchor, weight by height ([Sabu](https://alvarosabu.dev/blog/animating-grass-with-wind-in-tsl/)). Fish already do this; palms and the hut's planks do not.
8. Vertex animation textures are the path for many skinned characters (a crowd at an opening day, a flock of authored critters): `three-vat` bakes glTF clips to textures with a TSL decoder for `WebGPURenderer`, MIT, three ≥ 0.186 ([three-vat](https://github.com/MikeFernandez-Pro/three-vat)). three.js 0.187 also ships `webgpu_skinning_instancing`. Not needed until there are dozens of skinned things on screen.
9. Vehicles are code end to end: raycast suspension on the body, then a visual layer of spring bounce, roll into turns, pitch on acceleration, wheel spin from distance travelled ([Rapier](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/python/vehicle_controller); [cannon-es example](https://github.com/icurtis1/raycast-vehicle)). Trains add pure kinematics: side rods and pistons from wheel angle, a little body sway and buffer slack.
10. The smallest prototypes, in order: a shared `spring.ts`/`ease.ts` and port `feel.ts` to it; hut pieces with overshoot and a fly-in arc; wheel spin and rod kinematics on the wagon; palm and plank wind in `positionNode`; foot spheres on the avatar; one authored clip (a wave) through the glTF pipeline to prove the clip path.

## Findings by source

### Springs and dampers: make one exact implementation and use it everywhere

Daniel Holden's "Spring-It-On" is the most practical reference ([theorangeduck.com](https://theorangeduck.com/page/spring-roll-call)). The common `x = lerp(x, goal, k)` is frame-rate dependent, and scaling `k` by `dt` blows up once `k·dt > 1`. The fix is to parameterise by **half-life**: `x = lerp(x, goal, 1 − 2^(−dt/halflife))`, which is exact and stable at any `dt`. For springs, explicit or semi-implicit Euler (`v += dt·(k·(g−x) − d·v); x += dt·v`) diverges at large steps or stiff settings; the closed-form solution for under-, critically and over-damped springs does not. His conversions are worth keeping: `stiffness = (2πf)²`, damping ratio `ζ = d / (2√k)`, and `damping = 4·ln2 / halflife` for a critically damped spring. He also gives a **timed spring** (reach the goal at about a chosen time, which suits "this piece lands in 0.4 s") and **inertialization** (when a target jumps, keep the offset and decay it to zero, so switching from one animation source to another never pops). He finds critically damped springs look nicer than cubic splines for short blends ([X](https://x.com/anorangeduck/status/1304207460278898688)). Alexis Bacot compares the same family to Unity's `SmoothDamp` ([blog](https://www.alexisbacot.com/blog/the-art-of-damping)).

The prior report already covers t3ssel8r's second-order dynamics (frequency, damping, initial response) and its large-step caveat; Holden's closed forms are the answer to that caveat. For Boogle: `feel.ts` uses `k = 260`, `damp = 14`, which is about **2.6 Hz at ζ ≈ 0.43**, stepped with semi-implicit Euler. That is stable at the 50 ms frame cap (ω·dt ≈ 0.8), but the soapbox (`k = 180`, `d = 12`) and any future stiffer spring will not be guaranteed so; an exact solver removes the question.

### Few poses, code places them: Overgrowth, Spore, Ubisoft IK Rig

David Rosen's GDC 2014 talk "An Indie Approach to Procedural Animation" shows Overgrowth, Receiver and Black Shades animated with "very few key frames" ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1020583/Animation-Bootcamp-An-Indie-Approach); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-an-indie-approach-to-procedural-animation); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/GDC2014Rosen)). I did not watch the video. Secondary sources report about 13 key frames in total, walk and run cycles of four poses each (pass and reach per side), with bones interpolated between them, and the principle "responsiveness lives in the physics, animation is layered on top" ([Unity forum](https://discussions.unity.com/t/an-indie-approach-to-procedural-animation-gdc-video-talk/538228); [Little Polygon](https://blog.littlepolygon.com/posts/loco1/)). Little Polygon, who used key-pose "stances" on Solar Ash, rebuilds the idea in Unreal: the body simulates first, then code offsets a reference pose. Phase advances with normalised speed (`phase += 2.0 · speed01 · dt`); hip bob is `sin(2π·phase)` with a downward bias; hip roll runs at half that frequency for the left-right cadence; lean comes from acceleration, capped at 45° and smoothed by a 0.25 s spring; the bob's amplitude follows stick tilt through a 0.5 s spring. The author calls his own lean exaggerated for the demo.

Spore had the harder problem: animating creatures whose shape was unknown at authoring time. Its tool, Spasm, stored animation in a morphology-independent form, as IK targets "relative to the body", and a team of testers checked each animation on extreme bodies ([SIGGRAPH paper](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/216813518_Real-time_motion_retargeting_to_highly_varied_user-created_morphologies); [Game Anim](https://www.gameanim.com/2008/08/10/spore-animation-white-paper/); Hecker's own pages were down when I tried them). Ubisoft's IK Rig (Bereznyak, GDC 2016) generalised the same idea: convert any animation to IK chains, adjust them in context (uneven ground, carrying, touching another character) and convert back to any rig ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023279/IK-Rig-Procedural-Pose); [Game Anim](https://www.gameanim.com/2018/02/03/ik-rig-procedural-pose-animation/)); one reviewer notes the talk's examples were pre-rendered.

The lesson for a small team is the split, not the tech: a few authored poses or clips carry the character, and code puts them on the ground, aims them and adds the inertia.

### Procedural bodies: Rain World, Grow Home, Baluchon

Rain World's slugcat is two circles held at a fixed distance, which lets it tumble; limbs and tail are drawn on top and animated in code from input. Jakobsson describes the method as placing points and connecting them at set distances, and says he moved from a classical/procedural mix to mostly procedural ([Unity blog](https://unity.com/blog/exploring-procedural-design-rain-world); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/art/video-animating-i-rain-world-i-and-its-many-squishy-stretchy-creatures); the GDC 2016 talk itself I did not watch). This is the cheapest rig there is: a chain of points with distance constraints, which suits tails, scarves, a carried log bundle's rope, or the train's coupling chains.

Grow Home began as an internal tool for exploring procedural animation; BUD's foot strikes and limb flops are computed live, his hands are steered independently, and the team redesigned levels around a character that can climb anything ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/surrendering-control-without-letting-go-procedural-character-movement); [Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7fDmgKCCwWJQtyoVoOvvGM/get-ready-to-grow-home)). The same article's caveat is the one the prior report made: procedural movement trades precision for surprise, and players must learn the body.

For a capsule with no skeleton, Baluchon's walk is the right size ([itch.io devlog](https://piecesgames.itch.io/baluchon/devlog/193685/procedural-walking-system)). Its feet are separate objects. A coroutine alternates feet; the moving foot's goal is a raycast hit a little ahead along the facing; a curve lifts it on the way; everything scales with speed, so a still character keeps its feet planted and a fast one takes longer, higher steps. Boogle can skip the raycast and use `groundHeight(x, z)`.

### Ragdolls: the TABS recipe and why it stays out

Landfall's units in Totally Accurate Battle Simulator are ragdolls with torque on the legs from animation curves, a step handler that decides when a step is done, a standing handler that pushes the torso and head up by the head's height above the floor, and a balance script that moves the feet under the body ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/how-landfall-games-finds-the-fun-in-physics-engines)). The marionette variant pulls a hidden centre-of-mass point upward ([640Lab](https://medium.com/@640Lab/active-ragdoll-physics-puppeteer-marionette-balance-technique-a2c09a4d8d6a); [Jacasch](https://medium.com/@jacasch/balancing-of-active-ragdolls-in-games-367f146b25fb)). Landfall's own designer says the TABS code would be unplayable in their shooter because the view would bounce and spin, so they wrote a separate system. Fall Guys' ragdolls were a deliberate comedy choice that the designer says had to be balanced against performance; I found no technical source on how the beans are built ([Fall Guys wiki summary](https://ultimatepopculture.fandom.com/wiki/Fall_Guys)). The prior report's conclusion stands: Boogle gets the laugh from canned flops synced as one number (`fl` in `feel.ts`), at no cost to control or bandwidth. A **fake ragdoll** (a capsule tumbling on a spring back to upright, with stubby feet trailing on a point chain) is the code-only middle ground.

### Squash and stretch: volume, velocity, and the GPU

Volume-preserving squash scales one axis by `s` and the other two by `1/√s` ([Rohmer et al., velocity skinning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.04934)); Pixar's patent blends between full and no volume preservation with one amount ([US7221379](https://patents.google.com/patent/US7221379)). Without it, objects read as growing and shrinking rather than deforming. Velocity skinning computes per-vertex squash and "floppy" lag from each bone's linear and angular velocity, with painted weights for where it applies, and runs in a vertex shader. For Boogle's rigid props (a log, a crate, a hut plank) the same idea is one uniform per instance: a velocity vector that stretches the mesh along motion and bends its far end back. The game-feel skill's rule applies to all of it: exaggerate briefly and return to rest, and scale the effect to the event.

### Tweens and easing curves

Robert Penner's easing family (sine, quad, cubic, back, elastic, bounce, in/out/in-out) is the shared vocabulary of every tween library. three.js 0.187 ships `tween.module.js` in its addons, and GSAP with all its plugins became free for commercial use after the Webflow acquisition in 2025, under a standard licence that is not open source ([Webflow](https://webflow.com/blog/gsap-becomes-free); [GSAP README](https://unpkg.com/gsap@3.13.0/README.md)). Neither is needed: Boogle animates in its own `onFrame` loop, and a tween is a start time, a duration and a curve, which is a dozen lines per curve. Roblox build animations use the same pattern, tweening each part in turn with an elastic ease for the drop ([DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/tycoon-animations/833635)). A timed spring (above) is the better choice wherever a target can change mid-flight.

### Construction you can watch

The literature is thin. Townscaper places a block at once and fills in its detail a moment later, with the delay growing as structures get complex ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/how-townscaper-works-a-story-four-games-in-the-making)); Tiny Glade's tower roofs show a "tile-jumping" effect when raised fast ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/a-look-at-procedural-tower-roofs-in-tiny-glade)). I found no developer write-up of either game's placement animation. The one method write-up, for 2D city builders, bakes five build-up frames per building from a shared kit of scaffolding, beams and walls, painted over a finished render from foundation to roof, and finished 40 buildings in a few days ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/city-builder-games-make-build-up-animations-cheaply)). Its rules transfer: progress from foundation upward, respect the real structure, and reuse one kit. An open-source city builder holds build animations on their first frame under Reduce Motion ([citybuilder PR](https://github.com/cedricziel/citybuilder/pull/226)); Boogle's `reducedMotion` constant can do the same.

The hut in `src/proto/build/` is already the right architecture (a blueprint as data, a Local view that drops pieces as lockstep stock arrives). Its motion is a quadratic fall with a squash settle, its scaffolding rises and tips. What the sources add: an overshoot settle (back or elastic ease, or an underdamped spring) reads as "placed", a fly-in on an arc from the delivering wagon or pile ties cause to effect, and a short delay between one piece and the next (Townscaper's fill-in) makes the order readable.

### Vertex animation in TSL: wind, wobble, critters

Álvaro Sabu's TSL grass puts wind in `positionNode`: a two-octave `mx_noise_float` field over world XZ and time, sampled once per instance at its anchor so a blade moves as one, with a positive bias so it leans downwind, multiplied by height and by a per-vertex "tipness" (0 at the base, 1 at the tip), plus a droop term so the tip drops as it bends instead of stretching ([blog](https://alvarosabu.dev/blog/animating-grass-with-wind-in-tsl/)). ABZÛ animated thousands of fish with vertex animation along the spine (translation, yaw and a travelling wave) ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024409/Creating-the-Art-of-ABZU); [Game Anim](https://www.gameanim.com/2018/05/19/animating-thousands-of-fish-in-abzu/)), which `critter-fish.ts` already does through `patchNode`. The stylized-rendering report has the mobile costs (vertex-bound on weak devices, zero added draws on merged meshes).

### GPU-driven skinned crowds: vertex animation textures

`three-vat` bakes a glTF `AnimationClip` into textures once (in a worker or offline with a CLI) and draws a crowd as one `InstancedMesh` per material; each instance has a clip, a start time and a speed, and the GPU decodes the pose ([GitHub](https://github.com/MikeFernandez-Pro/three-vat)). It has a TSL decoder for `WebGPURenderer` and needs three ≥ 0.186 (Boogle is on 0.187). Its "rig" encoding is about two orders of magnitude smaller than per-vertex encoding. Limits: no runtime IK or multi-clip blending, discrete frames, texture size caps (pass the device's max; mobile often caps at 4096), and an earlier release silently dropped the crowd on WebGPU when a pipeline was invalid. A Blender add-on bakes simulations (cloth, particles) to VAT or storage buffers for three.js ([VAT-blender-addon](https://github.com/flement/VAT-blender-addon)). three.js's own `webgpu_skinning_instancing` example is the alternative when instances need real skinning ([three.js examples](https://github.com/mrdoob/three.js/blob/dev/examples/files.json)).

### Vehicles

Rapier's vehicle controller is one rigid body whose wheels are rays pushing along suspension springs ([Rapier](https://rapier.rs/docs/user_guides/python/vehicle_controller)); the three.js/cannon-es RC car sample does the same with no wheel bodies, which is what keeps it stable ([raycast-vehicle](https://github.com/icurtis1/raycast-vehicle)). Wheels are visual only: placed where the ray hits, spun by distance, steered by angle ([Raycer](https://github.com/Ransomwave/Raycer)). Toyful's Very Very Valet cars use the same raycast model with three forces per tyre, tuned arcade and bouncy, including letting the suspension push past its rest length ([Toyful](https://www.toyfulgames.com/blog/deep-dive-car-physics); [Unity forum](https://discussions.unity.com/t/incredibly-good-deep-dive-into-car-physics/907520); I did not watch the video). A lean controller's stiffness and damping should be tuned by feel, not read as a frequency ([EdenSpark](https://docs.edenspark.io/index/vehicle_controller_component.html)). The soapbox already layers a landing-kicked spring, roll from lateral acceleration and pitch from forward acceleration over its sphere body; the cannon-es numbers are in progression-feel-exploration.md.

Trains need no physics at all in Boogle (their position is a function of the tick). Every moving part is kinematics: wheel angle = distance / radius, side-rod and piston positions from that angle, bogie yaw from the two points on the track (already done), plus cosmetic body sway, a buffer-slack spring between cars on start and stop, and chimney puffs on each piston stroke.

### Where clips beat code

Clips win where the motion is a performance: a specific gesture timed to a sound, a character's acting, a cutscene beat, a dance at an opening day. House House's animator describes the Goose's look as slapstick and pantomime, with big reactions nobody can miss (interviews at [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behind-the-honk-an-i-untitled-goose-game-i-q-a) and [Road to the IGF](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/road-to-the-igf-house-house-s-i-untitled-goose-game-i-), via search summaries); I found no source saying whether the goose itself was hand-keyed or procedural. Clips also win when an artist must judge every pose, and when a Blender simulation (a collapsing scaffold, cloth) is easier to bake than to code. three.js 0.187 supports skinning and morph targets under WebGPU and has `AnimationMixer` crossfades and additive layers (`AnimationUtils.makeClipAdditive`); the additive example is WebGL, so additive layers on WebGPU need a check. Holden's inertialization is the cheap way to hand over between a clip and code without a pop.

## What this means for Boogle

Recommendations only; the owner decides. Each names the DESIGN.md principle it would serve, where the work goes and the smallest prototype that tests it.

1. **One animation maths module, in code.** Add a three.js-free `src/anim/` (or extend `math-pure.ts`) with Holden's half-life damper, an exact critically damped spring, an underdamped spring with frequency and ζ, a timed spring, inertialization, and six easing curves (outQuad, outCubic, outBack, outElastic, outBounce, inOutSine). Unit-test the closed forms against a fine-step Euler integration. *Smallest prototype:* port `feel.ts`'s squash spring to it with the same 2.6 Hz / ζ 0.43 and check with `/look` that the landing looks identical at 60 fps and at a forced 20 fps. Serves 10 (feel), and every item below.

2. **Avatars: stay in code, add feet and volume.** Keep the capsule's jelly layer in code. Make squash volume-preserving (`1/√s` on x and z), add a look-at for the eyes toward the nearest interactable, and try two foot spheres that step Baluchon-style onto `groundHeight` ahead of the body, phase-locked to speed, planted when still. A tumble (spin on a spring back to upright) extends the `fl` flop vocabulary without ragdoll physics. *Smallest prototype:* `?proto=feet`, two spheres and a step rule in one feature file, Local only (remotes derive steps from their smoothed velocity, as the spring layer already does). Serves 10.

3. **Characters with a performance: clips, few of them.** If the player or The Boogler gets an authored body (open question 7 in boogle-2.md), author a small pose set in Blender (idle, a wave, a cheer, a carry pose) and let code do locomotion, lean and squash on top, the Overgrowth split. *Smallest prototype:* one rigged greybox with a single "wave" clip through the glTF pipeline, played by `AnimationMixer` on a key, crossfaded back to the code-driven pose with inertialization, measured with `npm run perf -- --render`. This proves the clip path before anyone models a character.

4. **Construction: code tweens over the blueprint, clips only for the set piece.** Keep the hut as data plus a Local view. Add an outBack or underdamped-spring settle per piece kind (planks bounce, posts thud, roof panels flap), a fly-in on an arc from the nearest delivery point for some pieces, a Townscaper-style short delay before trims and the sign, and a wobble on the scaffold when a player lands on it. Hold pieces still under `reducedMotion`. Keep a baked Blender animation for one-off set pieces (an opening-day reveal) if the owner wants one. *Smallest prototype:* swap `index.ts`'s quadratic fall and squash for the shared curves and a per-kind table, compare motion sheets before and after. Serves 7 (telegraph: the order of pieces explains the build) and 9.

5. **Vehicles: all code, visual layer on top of the body.** For the wagon: wheel spin from distance, side rods and piston from wheel angle, a little roll on curves, a buffer-slack spring between cars when the train starts and stops, puffs on piston strokes. For the soapbox: move its spring to the shared module and add wheel compression (each wheel's height follows `groundHeight` under it, so the chassis visibly rides bumps). *Smallest prototype:* rods and wheel spin on the greybox loco in `look.ts`, driven from the tick so every tab agrees for free. Serves 1 (riding is the toy).

6. **Wind and wobble in `positionNode`.** Palms, the hut's loose planks and scaffold flags, and laundry-type props sway from one shared wind uniform and a per-vertex sway weight, sampled per instance at the anchor. *Smallest prototype:* `patchNode` on the props material with a tip weight from vertex height, one uniform for strength; check draw calls stay flat with `npm run perf -- --render`. Serves 9 (an island that moves while you stand still).

7. **Critters: vertex animation first, VAT only for skinned crowds.** Fish already wiggle in the shader; gulls' wing flaps and any crab legs should follow the same pattern. Bring in `three-vat` only if a scene needs dozens of skinned, clip-animated characters at once (an opening-day crowd of townsfolk). *Smallest prototype, when that day comes:* bake one Mixamo-style clip, draw 200 instances with random start times on the iPad, read frame time; check the WebGPU path on the current release first.

8. **Point chains for floppy bits.** A Rain World-style chain of points with distance constraints, in code, for tails, scarves, the haul bundle's strap, coupling chains and a kite string. *Smallest prototype:* a five-point strap on the back bundle, drawn as one tube or a few boxes, Local.

9. **Keep active ragdolls out.** The TABS recipe works but costs control and, in a networked game, a pose per limb; the canned flop events already give the laugh. Revisit only if a mode is built around losing control.

10. **No tween library.** `tween.module.js` and GSAP are available, but a loop-owned tween is a few lines with the shared curves, and it stays in Boogle's frame phases and pause rules.

Open questions for playtests and the owner: how much overshoot construction pieces can take before it reads as flimsy; whether foot spheres help or clutter a capsule at phone size; and whether the first authored character comes before or after the find-the-fun steps.
