# Grow every room its own island, in integers

Boogle can generate a fresh, seed-deterministic island per room cheaply and safely if it builds the whole pipeline from integer fields and integer cellular automata: a falloff-shaped "continentalness" field passed through a hand-tuned lookup curve (Minecraft 1.18's idea at toy scale), a short pass of Jacob Olsen's speed-optimised thermal erosion, integer Priority-Flood to decide lakes and guarantee drainage, and a spring placed at the head of the longest drainage path so the existing water sim produces a stream that reaches the sea. At Boogle's 129 x 129 grid, a prototype of exactly that pipeline ran in **about 7 ms warm (21 ms cold) on an Apple M1 Max**, so even a slow phone finishes in well under a quarter second, once, at room join, with no worker. The real risks are not speed but determinism and persistence: transcendental `Math` functions are not reproducible across browsers ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Math)), and generator edits silently change maps ([mapgen4](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/mapgen4/)), which would shift terrain under a persistent garden. Heavier techniques the brief names (particle or pipe-model hydraulic erosion, stream-power uplift, wave function collapse) are either float-bound, built for terrains a thousand times larger, or a poor fit for a smooth diggable heightfield; WFC is worth keeping only for optional prop clusters. The rest of this report shows how the references support that design, where it bends DESIGN.md, and what to build first.

## Three shaped fields beat clever noise for a 160-metre island

Every reference generator reduces to a few low-frequency fields remapped through curves an artist tunes. Red Blob Games sums octaves of noise, reshapes the result with a power curve, and turns it into an island by blending with a distance term, `e = lerp(e, 1 − d, mix)`, where `d` is a square-bump or squared-Euclidean distance from the centre ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)). Minecraft Java 1.18 does the same thing at continental scale: **three 2D noises (continentalness, erosion, peaks-and-valleys) go through splines to produce a height offset and a vertical stretch**, with higher continentalness raising land and higher erosion flattening it, and beach and ocean biomes depending only on continentalness and temperature ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/World_generation)). Biomes in both systems are table lookups: Red Blob indexes a Whittaker-style table by elevation and a moisture noise and warns the thresholds are just examples to tune ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/terrain-from-noise/)), and Minecraft picks the nearest biome interval in a six-dimensional parameter space ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/World_generation)).

That structure ports to integers almost for free. Squared distance needs no `sqrt`, a piecewise-linear spline is a small integer table with fixed-point interpolation, and the `pow` redistribution is another table. Boogle already has the right primitive: `hash2` in `src/math.js` is pure `Math.imul` integer hashing, although its `valueNoise` and `fbm` interpolate in floats and `src/adapters/terrain.js` shapes the island with `Math.exp` and `Math.hypot` before rounding to centimetres. For one hand-built island every peer computes the same floats from the same code, so the risk is small; with a new island per seed, every peer generates a different map in every room, and any cell whose float lands near a half-centimetre boundary can round differently. Boogle already banned native trig in the shared sim for this reason (commit `0b0b834`), and the generator should follow the same rule.

For a beach toy, the fields worth having are: a **coast field** (an ellipse with seeded radii and centre jitter, its edge wobbled by low-frequency integer fbm) that decides sea, shallows, beach and inland; a **roughness field** that decides flat beach versus dunes and a hill; and a **moisture** proxy, read from the hydrology pass below rather than from noise, that decides grass versus sand colour and where mushrooms like to grow. Red Blob's own polygon generator derives moisture from breadth-first distance to lakes and rivers rather than from noise ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/polygon-map-generation/)), which suits an island whose water you can see. No Man's Sky shows the escape hatch when simulation is too expensive: Hello Games approximated erosion's results procedurally because true erosion was infeasible for runtime-local generation ([GDC 2017 notes](https://blather-rinse-repeat.blogspot.com/2018/11/gdc-2017-part-ii-main-conference-notes.html)). Boogle has the opposite situation, a tiny grid generated once, so it can afford real, cheap erosion.

## Olsen's 2004 erosion rule is already Boogle's water rule

The erosion literature splits into three tiers. At the top, stream-power methods model tectonic uplift against river incision over a stream graph ([Cordonnier et al. 2016](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf12820)), and the 2024 analytical variant makes erosion time a parameter you can jump to directly ([Tzathas et al. 2024](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1111/cgf15033)). They produce mountain ranges, which is far beyond what a 160 m island needs. In the middle sit hydraulic simulations: Mei, Decaudin and Hu's GPU "virtual pipes" shallow-water model carries terrain, water, sediment, flux and velocity textures ([hgpu](https://hgpu.org/?p=2256)), and droplet erosion after Beyer tracks particles with inertia, capacity and evaporation ([erodr](https://github.com/henrikglass/erodr)). Both are float-heavy by nature, and making them bit-exact across peers would cost more than they add here.

The bottom tier is the useful one. Olsen took Musgrave's 1989 thermal erosion and made it **six times faster** with four changes: use four neighbours instead of eight, move material only to the lowest neighbour, move half the height difference, and write results in place instead of buffering. That cut 500 iterations on a 512² map from 60 s to 10 s on 2004 hardware, and he found **50 iterations enough** to show the effect ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)). His proposed algorithm is the same loop with the talus test inverted (`0 < dmax ≤ T`), which levels gentle ground and leaves cliffs alone. It produced flat, usable areas plus crisp slopes, and it shipped in Tribal Trouble, where synthesis and erosion took 2 s for 512² maps ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)). That is close to Boogle's own water rule in `src/sim/water.js`: a fixed scan order, live in-place updates, and moving at most half the difference so a pair of cells never swaps order. An integer sand-slump pass with a talus in centimetres inherits the same determinism argument. Run the normal version on dunes to soften noise into slumped sand, and the inverted version near the court and garden to make flat play areas.

The prototype measurement makes the cost concrete. Integer shaping plus fbm took 0.8 ms, 50 thermal iterations 3.8–4.1 ms, Priority-Flood 0.5–1.8 ms and flow accumulation about 2 ms, for **7–9 ms per seed warm**, with identical hashes on repeat runs (this report's own measurement, Node 24 on an M1 Max). An earlier version that re-sorted its priority queue on every pop and allocated neighbour arrays per cell took 37–60 ms. At this size, data-structure hygiene matters more than the choice of algorithm. No phone was measured; assuming a 4–10x scalar-JS slowdown puts generation at roughly 30–90 ms.

## Priority-Flood makes the spring's stream reach the sea

Boogle's integer water sim is the ground truth for where water goes, so worldgen does not need to draw rivers. It only needs to guarantee that a spring's water has somewhere sensible to go, and that is a solved problem in hydrology. **Priority-Flood** floods inward from the map edge with a priority queue, raising each unvisited neighbour to at least the current level. It is **O(n) on integer heights**, about 20 lines of pseudocode, works on 4-connected grids, and has variants that leave a strictly descending path from every cell (+ε) and that label every outlet's catchment ([Barnes, Lehman & Mulla 2014](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1511.04463)). With integer heights the queue becomes an array of buckets, one per centimetre. The +ε variant becomes "+1 cm per step", but Boogle's water rule never moves water across a 1 cm difference, so a carved channel needs a drop of at least 2 cm per cell or the stream stalls on a staircase.

Red Blob's drainage model supplies the rest: point every cell at its lowest neighbour, accumulate flow, and pick river sources from filtered candidates (high, steep, wet), noting that the seed and traversal order choose among many valid drainage graphs ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/polygon-map-generation/)). In integer form that order must be fixed (sort by height, then index), which the prototype does. One finding from the prototype matters for feel: **filling every pit on a noisy island produced 800–1,300 lake cells per seed**, flat sheets of water that would eat the beach. The better policy is to breach small depressions by carving their outlet and keep only the one or two largest as deliberate ponds. Then place the spring at the head of the longest drainage path that ends in the sea or in a kept pond, and seed it with `setSource`. Placing the spring this way is inference from the drainage literature; I found no game source describing spring placement specifically.

## Blue-noise slots, not WFC, for points of interest and secrets

Points of interest and secrets need an even spread, terrain filters and stability as features are added. Red Blob recommends blue noise for object placement because it avoids both clumps and gaps, with Poisson-disc sampling as the simplest generator ([Red Blob Games](https://www.redblobgames.com/articles/noise/introduction.html)); Bridson's algorithm produces it in O(N) ([Bridson 2007](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~rbridson/docs/bridson-siggraph07-poissondisk.pdf)). Minecraft's structure placement is the most transferable pattern: one attempt per grid cell of size `spacing`, offset by a seeded jitter bounded by `separation`, a per-structure-set integer **salt**, a frequency roll, exclusion zones between sets, and terrain or biome vetoes afterwards ([Minecraft Wiki](https://minecraft.wiki/w/Structure_set)). The salt is the important part for a friends' codebase where agents add features in parallel: each feature draws from `mulberry32(seed ^ salt)`, so adding a "message in a bottle" never moves the existing tide pools. Secrets fit DESIGN.md's "Leave something for the next visitor" and "Toys before chores" when they are things you stumble on by digging, such as items buried only in cells the dig mask allows. For guarantees, Olsen's advice still holds: open-ended retry loops are too costly, so tune the generator until its criteria hold in about 99% of seeds ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)), and use a small, bounded number of deterministic rerolls (seed + attempt) as a safety net.

Wave function collapse looks tempting because of Oskar Stålberg's island dioramas, but it suits a different problem. WFC repeatedly collapses the lowest-entropy cell and propagates adjacency constraints. It can hit contradictions, although they are "surprisingly rare" ([mxgmn](https://github.com/mxgmn/WaveFunctionCollapse)), and because its constraints are local it "rarely produces large-scale structure" ([Boris the Brave](https://www.boristhebrave.com/2020/04/13/wave-function-collapse-explained/)). Bad North used it to assemble handcrafted tiles into islands with a navigability heuristic, and Townscaper paired it with marching cubes on an irregular grid ([mxgmn](https://github.com/mxgmn/WaveFunctionCollapse); [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/using-wave-function-collapse-algorithm-for-dioramas)). Boogle's terrain is a smooth sand heightfield that players dig and pile at cell resolution, and tile-based terrain would fight that. If WFC appears at all, it should be for optional decor such as rock clusters or a boardwalk. Keeping it deterministic then needs integer weights, the "fewest remaining options" heuristic instead of float entropy, lowest-index tie-breaks, and a restart with seed + 1 on contradiction. I could not access a transcript of Stålberg's talks, so Bad North's tileset size and contradiction handling are unverified.

## Prioritized recommendations

| # | Recommendation | Why | DESIGN.md principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Make generation **integer-only** (Imul hashing, fixed-point fbm, squared distances, lookup-table curves; no `Math.exp/hypot/pow/sin`). Port today's island to it first and confirm it looks right. | Cross-browser `Math` precision differs ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/JavaScript/Reference/Global_Objects/Math)) | Determinism from day one |
| 2 | **Pin the island to the room**: seed = FNV-1a hash of the room name; store `{seed, genVersion}` in the persisted snapshot; keep `beach` on today's island; check a hash of the base heightfield on join. | Generator edits change maps ([mapgen4](https://www.redblobgames.com/maps/mapgen4/)); gardens persist | Leave something for the next visitor; Designed for drop-in |
| 3 | **Generator returns anchors** (court, spawn, ball spawn, garden patch, spring) instead of constants in `constants.js`/`mushrooms.js`. | Several modules hard-code positions on the hand-built island | Small and readable |
| 4 | **Hydrology pass**: bucket-queue Priority-Flood, breach small pits and keep 1–2 ponds, carve the spring's path with at least 2 cm of drop per cell, spring at the head of the longest sea-bound path. | O(n) on integers ([Barnes et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1511.04463)); water must flow | Toys before chores (water is the glue) |
| 5 | **Olsen erosion** in integer cm: about 30–50 normal passes for dunes; the inverted rule to flatten the court and garden. | 50 iterations suffice ([Olsen 2004](https://web.mit.edu/cesium/Public/terrain.pdf)) | Slapstick (soft sand) |

### Sketch: per-room islands in Boogle

The generator is a pure module, `src/sim/island-gen.js` (no three.js, no DOM, so it is unit-testable), that returns typed arrays and anchors. Today `src/adapters/terrain.js` and `src/world/terrain-edits.js` build the island at import time. They would instead call the generator once `world.seed` is known: locally, or from the host's welcome snapshot for joiners. The water region and mushroom patch read the returned anchors.

```js
// src/sim/island-gen.js — integer-only. Returns cm heights + anchors. GEN_VERSION bumps on any output change.
export const GEN_VERSION = 1;
export function generateIsland(seed, n = 129) {
  const rng = (salt) => mulberry32((seed ^ salt) >>> 0);         // one stream per stage/feature
  const H = new Int16Array(n * n);
  // 1. Shape: seeded ellipse (centre jitter, radii) on squared distance d2 (x1024), coast wobble from integer fbm,
  //    height = CURVE_LUT[d2 + roughness] (piecewise-linear table: deep sea -750 → shallows -120 → beach 0..160 → hill).
  // 2. Hill/dune blobs from rng(SALT_HILL): integer cones or quadratic bumps, scaled by the roughness field.
  // 3. Erosion: thermal(H, talus=40cm, 40 passes), lowest-neighbour, in place, move (d-T)>>1;
  //    then inverted pass (flatten d<=T) inside the court and garden discs.
  // 4. Hydrology: F = priorityFloodBuckets(H, sea=0); breach pits with area < A_MIN; keep the largest 1-2 as ponds;
  //    receivers + flow accumulation in fixed (height, index) order;
  //    spring = argmax(path length to sea) among cells with H in [250, 600] cm; carve path with >= 2 cm drop per cell.
  // 5. Anchors: court = flattest beach disc near the spring's mouth; garden = flat inland patch near the stream;
  //    spawn/ball next to the court; POIs and secrets = jittered-grid slots per feature salt, filtered by the dig mask.
  // 6. Validate (land area, court flatness, spring reaches sea, beach band size); on failure retry with seed+attempt (<= 4).
  return { version: GEN_VERSION, heights: H, court, spawn, ballSpawn, garden, spring, pois, hash: fnv(H) };
}
```

On the network side nothing new travels except two integers. The room's seed comes from hashing the room name. The host's snapshot already carries `seed` and would add `genVersion` and a `baseHash`. A joiner whose regenerated `baseHash` disagrees asks for the base heightfield once (33 KB raw, compressible) rather than desyncing. That fallback also lets old rooms keep their island after a generator change, if the old generator version is not kept in the code. A seed-sweep unit test in `test/` (200 seeds at a reduced grid, well under a second) guards determinism and the invariants, and `npm run look` stills of a handful of seeds judge the looks. Generation itself is shared-state work, so the change ships through `check:full`.

## Conclusion

The research reverses a common instinct: at Boogle's scale the hard part of procedural islands is not realism or speed. It is keeping a generated world fixed: identical across browsers, and identical across months of code changes under a garden that friends keep coming back to. That pushes the design toward the oldest, simplest tools (integer hashing, lookup curves, Olsen's 2004 automaton, a bucket-queue flood) and away from the impressive ones. A useful side effect is that the generator and the live sim then share one vocabulary. The erosion pass is the same kind of integer automaton as the water rule, and the hydrology pass's promise that a stream reaches the sea is checked by the same water sim the players use. That suggests a cheap, distinctive next step after per-room islands: let a short, deterministic "pre-run" of the real water sim settle each new island's spring before anyone arrives, so every fresh room opens with a stream already running to the sea and its ponds already full, and the generator can reject any seed where that does not happen.
